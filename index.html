1. Problem and introduction

Requirement: a cooling fan must run at a speed chosen by a setting (a potentiometer). Instead of wasting power in a series resistor, we switch the motor fully ON and OFF at a fixed frequency and vary the duty cycle D. The motor's inertia and inductance average the pulses, so speed is roughly proportional to the average voltage Vavg = D·VCC.

The 555 in astable mode generates the pulse train. Two diodes steer the capacitor's charge and discharge currents through different parts of one potentiometer, so the frequency stays constant while duty changes. A MOSFET buffers the output and switches the motor.

Prerequisites: RC charging, comparators, SR latch, MOSFET switching. Terms: duty cycle (tH/T), PWM, flyback diode, Vavg.

3. Working principle

  1. Charging (output HIGH): C1 charges from 1/3 to 2/3 VCC through RA and one diode. Discharge pin is open.
  2. At 2/3 VCC: the upper comparator resets the latch, OUT goes LOW and the discharge transistor turns ON.
  3. Discharging (output LOW): C1 discharges from 2/3 to 1/3 VCC through the other diode and RB.
  4. At 1/3 VCC: the lower comparator sets the latch, OUT returns HIGH and the cycle repeats.
  5. OUT drives the MOSFET gate through R1: HIGH = motor ON, LOW = motor OFF. Turning the pot moves the wiper, trading RA against RB.

Misconceptions: (a) a plain astable 555 can never go below 50% duty, but the diode-steered version can; (b) PWM does not lower the voltage on the motor, it lowers the average; (c) speed vs. duty is only approximately linear because of friction and a starting threshold (the fan may stall at very low D).

4. Equations

tH = 0.693 · RA · C    tL = 0.693 · RB · C
T = tH + tL = 0.693 · (RA+RB) · C = 0.693 · RP · C    f = 1.44 / (RP C)
D = tH/T = RA / (RA+RB)    Vavg = D · VCC

Derivation: C charges toward VCC as v = VCC(1 − e−t/RC). From VCC/3 to 2VCC/3: t = RC·ln[(VCC−VCC/3)/(VCC−2VCC/3)] = RC ln 2 = 0.693RC. Discharge from 2/3 to 1/3 gives the same ln 2 factor with RB.

Units: R in Ω, C in F, t in s, f in Hz. Assumptions: diode drop (~0.6 V) and R3 are ignored; the real duty is slightly shifted, most noticeably near the extremes. Limits: D→0 when RA→0 (limited in practice by R3 and the diode); D→1 when RB→0. Frequency is independent of D, and independent of VCC because the thresholds scale with VCC.

7. Solved problems

Easy. RP = 100 kΩ, C = 10 nF. Find f. f = 1.44/(100×103×10×10−9) = 1.44/10−3 = 1.44 kHz, T = 0.693 ms.

Moderate. Fan speed wanted at about 30% with VCC = 12 V. D = 0.3 ⇒ RA = 30 kΩ, RB = 70 kΩ. tH = 0.693×30k×10 nF = 0.208 ms; tL = 0.485 ms; Vavg = 0.3×12 = 3.6 V.

GATE-style. A diode-steered astable 555 uses RA = 10 kΩ, RB = 40 kΩ, C = 0.1 µF (ideal diodes). Find f and D. T = 0.693×50k×0.1µF = 3.465 ms ⇒ f = 288.6 Hz; D = 10/50 = 20%. Shortcut: f = 1.44/(RPC) and D = RA/RP.

8. Design considerations and applications

  • Choose f above audible range (>~20 kHz) to avoid whining; this means a smaller RP C (e.g. 10 nF with ~7 kΩ total), or accept ~1.4 kHz for simplicity and noise.
  • Flyback diode across the motor is mandatory; add a bulk capacitor near the motor supply.
  • Check MOSFET VGS(th) against the 555 output (about VCC − 1.7 V) and the continuous drain current of the fan.
  • Applications: PC and server fans, power-supply cooling, heater and dimmer control. Roles: analog/power electronics, hardware design and validation.
  • Testing challenges: switching noise on the supply, stall at very low duty, and 555 output spikes at the transition.
